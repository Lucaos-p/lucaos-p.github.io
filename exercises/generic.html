The decade in four records

Hardcore Punk
in the 2010s

Four bands and four records that took hardcore in different directions between 2010 and 2019.

Ceremony Power Trip Turnstile Knocked Loose

Ceremony

Rohnert Park · 2010

Ceremony came out of Rohnert Park, California, and built its reputation on short, abrasive songs. As the band moved toward post-punk later in the decade, it showed how a group rooted in hardcore could change its sound without losing its intensity.

Brief bursts like “Back in '84” sit alongside longer, slower passages. That contrast gives the album room to explore suburban boredom and frustration without losing its bite.

Sources: Bridge Nine: album and release date; The Music: album review; Ceremony: later change in sound.

Power Trip

Nightmare Logic · 2017

Power Trip came out of the Texas underground and built on the crossover sound of its debut, Manifest Decimation. By matching hardcore's direct delivery with thrash metal's fast riffs, the band made crossover a meeting point for punk and metal listeners.

“Executioner's Tax (Swing of the Axe)” captures what made the record stand out: a riff you can recognise immediately and a chorus built to be shouted back.

Listen to the album

Sources: Power Trip: release date; Pitchfork: album review.

Turnstile

Time & Space · 2018

Turnstile built a following in Baltimore's hardcore scene before moving from its independent debut, Nonstop Feeling, to Roadrunner Records. Its mix of mosh riffs and danceable grooves showed how hardcore could reach listeners outside the scene.

The record pairs sharp, fast songs with melodic detours like “Moon.” Kerrang! named it album of the year, recognising how naturally those different sounds fit together.

Listen to the album

Sources: The Guardian: album review; Kerrang!: albums of the year.

Knocked Loose

A Different Shade of Blue · 2019

After making its name with Laugh Tracks, this Kentucky band pushed further into metallic hardcore. Its intense live shows and heavy breakdowns helped make that branch of hardcore more visible to metalcore audiences.

The second album uses dissonant guitar parts and sudden changes in pace to build tension before the breakdowns land. It shows how much variety the band could find within a relentlessly heavy sound.

Sources: Kerrang!: album review; RIFF Magazine: album review.
